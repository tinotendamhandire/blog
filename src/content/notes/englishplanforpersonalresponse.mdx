# Question and practice response

## Discuss how your response to a text was shaped by what its creator chose to include, emphasise or omit.

> "the sea isn't the same as the pool"

---

## Technique: Metaphor

**Winton shapes the reader's understanding of the girl by emphasising her physical and mechanical function** through an extended **metaphor** that equates her to a machine, specifically an engine — using this comparison to dramatise how she disciplines feelings into pure function.

The metaphor first appears as simple description, almost clinical in tone: *"she swam and thought the thoughts of a machine."* The comparison strips her of psychological complexity and reduces her to a mechanical process — swimming becomes not an emotional outlet but an operation, something performed rather than felt.

But as her rage towards her mother surfaces, the metaphor shifts from description into command, rendered through short, snappy **syntax**:

> *"Be an engine. Don't complain. Don't ask. Don't hesitate. Swim, don't think."*

The stacked imperatives, each a full stop away from the last, mimic an operating manual rather than a child's internal monologue. She is not simply comparing herself to a machine; she is instructing herself to become one. This suggests that her interiority hasn't vanished so much as been redirected.

The engine metaphor is the vocabulary used to convert unbearable feeling into something that can be controlled, switched on, run, and — most importantly — stopped.

Having grown up in an unstable household where the eldest or most capable child is expected to self-regulate rather than to be cared for, I recognise the pattern of a child converting emotional overwhelm into rigid self-control. This emphasis on mechanical command, and the omission of named feeling for the girl, shapes my response. Because the text withholds the word for what she feels, I find myself supplying it; reading her self-commands as a mask for grief and for fear. This unsettles me because the mask is efficient — there is no performance for the reader to hold onto — which leaves me anxious on her behalf.

## Planned Response Paragraph: Juxtaposition between the Mother and the Girl

|                | Emphasised                                    | Omitted                                       |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| **Mother**     | Named emotional states directly               | —                                             |
| **Girl**       | —                                             | Equivalent naming of her emotional state      |

### Examples:

- **Mother's emotion named:** *"sick bitter and afraid"*
- **Girl's physical description:** *"Tiny breasts"* / *"tall for her age"* / *"strong and hard"*

> *"All a person needs is a bit of land,"* she laughed, and it wasn't all derision.

*— the only time in the text that the girl is given the privilege of her own emotional response.*

---

### Land/Water Motif

- **Mother** = talks about land (stability) but drowns in liquid (explicitly alcohol, implicitly failure)
- **Girl** = doesn't need land; thrives in water (competence, escape)

> *"All a person needs is a bit of land."*
