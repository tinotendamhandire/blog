# Question and practice response
##### Discuss how <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">your response</mark> to a text was shaped by what its <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">creator chose to include, emphasise or omit.</mark>
"the sea isn't the same as the pool"

Technique. 
<mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">Winton shapes the readers understand of the girl by emphasing her physical and mechanical function</mark> through an extended <mark style="background: #FFB8EBA6;">metaphor</mark>, <mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;">that equates her to a machine, specifically an engine — using this comparison to dramatise how she discipline feelings into pure function.</mark>
<mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;">The metaphor first appears as simple description, almost clinical in tone: "she swam and though the thoughts of a machine." The comparison strips her of psychological complexity and reduces her to a mechanical process — swimming becomes not an emotional out but an operation, something performed rather than felt.
But as her rage towards her mother surfaces the metaphor shifts from description into command, rendered through short, snappy</mark> <mark style="background: #FFB8EBA6;">syntax</mark>. 
<mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;">"Be an engine. Don't complain. Don't ask. Don't hesitate. Swim, don't think.", The stacked imperatives, each a full stop away from the last, mimic an operating manual rather than a child's internal monolouge. 
She is not simply comparing herself to a machine, she is instruction herself to become one. This suggests that her interiority hasn't vanished so much as been redirected. </mark>
The engine metaphor is the vocabulary used to convert unbearable feeling into something that can be controlled, switched on, run, and most importantly stopped. 
Having grown around unstable household where the eldest or most capable child is expected to self regulate rather than to be cared for I recognise the pattern of a child converting emotional overwhelm into rigid self control. 
This emphasis on mechanical command, and the omission of named feeling for the girl shapes my response. 
Because the text withholds the word for what she feels <mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">I find myself supplying it; reading her self-commands as a mask for grief and for fear. this unsettles me because the mask is efficent, there is no performance for the reader to hold onto which leaves me anxious on her behalf.</mark> 

# Planned response paragraph
Juxtaposition between the mother and the girl 

|        | Emphasised                     | Omitted                                  |
| ------ | ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------- |
| Mother | Named emotional states directy | -                                        |
| Girl   | -                              | Equivalent naming of her emotional state |
Examples: 
Mother's emotion named -> "sick bitter and afraid"
Girl's physical description -> "Tiny breasts" / "tall for her age" / "strong and hard"
"All a person needs is a bit of land, She laughed and it wasn't all derision" <- only time in the text that the girl is given the privilege of her own emotional response 
##### Evidence (land/water motif)
"All a person needs is a bit of land"
Mother = Talks about land (stability) drowns in liquid (explicitly alcohol, implicitly failure)
Girl = doesn't need land; thrives in water (competence, escape)
##### Your response: (recognising + how do you feel about what you recognised)
Recognition: Asymmetry = objectification (daughter as body/utility not full subject)
Feelings (pick one and commit)
 - Discomfort - her worth being measured only in physical competence
 - Unease - that this asymmetry doesn't jar on first read (it feels normal which is in of itself, reoubling)
 - Ambivalence (flicker of hope) - The final laugh isn't full derision, so she isn't fully reduced.
